# DemandFoundry — Product Experience Blueprint

Transcribed from `DemandFoundry_Product_Experience_Blueprint 1.docx` (photographed pages, 15 Oct 2026).
Product concept & experience design. Combines **DemandFoundry** with **MissionLock**. Prepared for
Ashfaque Rifaye. Project codename: **Tesselle**.

> **Imagine it. Find a credible way to get it.**

## The idea in one sentence

A personal commerce agent that turns inspiration into a clear product brief, checks what can actually
be delivered, and helps the customer buy, source, customise or commission an item without losing the
requirements that mattered in the first place.

## The defining promise

Do not replace the customer's imagination with the nearest available product. Preserve their intent,
explain the trade-offs and find an honest path to fulfilment.

## Scope and reading note

This is a proposed product specification, not a description of an already operating service. All
screens, behaviours, commercial models and examples are design proposals. Illustrative prices,
dimensions and seller responses are fictional demonstration inputs, not verified market offers. Device
experiences are intended designs, not claims of current device support. Technical architecture,
implementation instructions, interfaces, endpoints, code, model selection and deployment are
deliberately excluded. The document does not assert patentability, market uniqueness, guaranteed
delivery or a guaranteed competition result.

## 01 · Product vision and boundaries

DemandFoundry serves the gap between an inspiring idea and a product the customer can genuinely obtain.

**The customer problem.** A customer imagines an accessory, organiser or improvement. A generated
concept makes the idea tangible, but existing products may differ in size, be unavailable locally, be
out of stock or fail an essential requirement. The customer either abandons the idea or buys a
compromise. The product makes those gaps explicit and creates a structured route to resolve them.

**Two capabilities, one experience**
- **MissionLock** is the customer-intent safeguard: purpose, must-haves, preferences, limits, unknowns
  and approved changes.
- **DemandFoundry** is the fulfilment experience: compare existing products, specialist sourcing,
  custom modifications and new production.
- The customer sees one project and one coherent journey, not separate tools or a collection of agents.

**Product principles**
- Inspiration is welcome; evidence is required before a commercial promise.
- A similar-looking product is not automatically a suitable product.
- Every material compromise is visible and requires explicit acceptance.
- The customer can buy one custom item without joining a shared batch.
- A project continues across surfaces without requiring the customer to repeat the brief.
- The product can honestly conclude that no credible fulfilment route is available.

**Initial category and expansion.** The demonstration focuses on **non-electrical desk organisers and
accessory holders**. Future categories may include non-structural home organisation, selected gardening
accessories and other bounded custom products after category-specific review. Electrical,
load-bearing, hazardous and safety-critical products are out of scope.

**What the product is not.** Not a universal manufacturing guarantee, an unlabelled image generator, an
unrestricted purchasing agent, a promise that every seller participates, or a mechanism for copying
protected branded designs. It does not infer precise measurements from an ordinary photograph or treat
a search miss as proof that a product does not exist.

## 02 · People, ecosystem and value

The customer owns the brief. Each participant contributes evidence or an offer without silently
changing that brief.

| Participant | Primary need | Proposed experience |
|---|---|---|
| Customer | Obtain the intended outcome | Explore, confirm requirements, compare routes, approve changes and accept delivery. |
| Household collaborator | Contribute without losing control | Comment on shared projects; only the designated purchaser approves spending. |
| Retailer | Convert suitable demand | Present deliverable stock with clear fit and availability information. |
| Sourcing specialist | Quote a credible acquisition route | Explain origin, total cost, delivery terms and unresolved conditions. |
| Maker or customisation seller | Receive a usable brief | Clarify feasibility, propose alternatives, quote and document the sample. |
| Fulfilment participant | Deliver the approved item | Provide milestones, shipment information and exception updates. |
| Support reviewer | Resolve discrepancies fairly | Compare the approved version, seller evidence and customer-reported issue. |

**Customer value.** Less repeated searching and explaining; clearer decisions; access to custom-making
where offered; visible costs and trade-offs; continuity from idea through aftercare. (Intended benefits
to validate, not measured results.)

**Seller value.** Better-qualified briefs, fewer ambiguous enquiries, clearer quote revisions and a way
to respond to demand that existing stock does not satisfy. Sellers can decline or propose changes
without being presented as having accepted the original brief.

**Commercial proposition.** Candidate revenue: a disclosed fee on completed sourcing or custom orders,
seller subscriptions for brief and quote management, optional design or sampling services. Any
customer-facing fee is visible before commitment. Paid relationships do not secretly determine
suitability rankings.

**Marketplace integrity.** Do not expose a customer's home imagery, contact details or full address to
a broad seller audience. Share only the necessary, approved brief. Show seller participation and
verification status precisely; a badge must describe what was checked rather than imply a blanket
guarantee.

## 03 · The full customer journey

Each stage has a clear destination, with an option to pause, revise or exit.

| Stage | Customer experience | Exit condition |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Inspire | Speak, type, photograph, upload or share an item. | A saved project with a clear purpose. |
| 2. Clarify | Confirm dimensions, essentials, preferences and limits. | A reviewable brief with unknowns exposed. |
| 3. Visualise | Refine labelled concept options. | A chosen direction, not a purchase promise. |
| 4. Explore routes | Compare buy, source and make options. | A preferred route or an honest no-match outcome. |
| 5. Request offers | Approve what sellers receive. | A sent request with status and next action. |
| 6. Compare and refine | Review quotes and deviations side by side. | A suitable, sufficiently evidenced offer. |
| 7. Approve | Confirm the exact version, price and terms. | A specific approved commitment. |
| 8. Fulfil | Follow samples, production and delivery. | The item is delivered or an exception is raised. |
| 9. Accept and resolve | Check the item against the approved brief. | Acceptance, remediation or a recorded dispute. |

**First visit.** Lead with *"What do you wish you could get?"* Offer four actions: **Describe it**,
**Show an inspiration**, **Capture my space** and **Continue a project**. Let the customer explore
before requesting personal details. Ask for a delivery region only when it affects results; request a
full address only when needed for a selected transaction.

**Returning visit.** Show projects needing a decision first, then active fulfilment and saved
inspiration. Each card answers: what changed, why it matters and what to do next. A customer should
never need to reread a long conversation to understand the current state.

**Pause and resume.** Save the latest confirmed brief, unresolved questions and selected concept. On
return, give a short recap: *"Your dimensions are confirmed. Two quotes need review."* A project may
stay exploratory indefinitely without pressure to order.

## 04 · Capture, clarify and protect intent

MissionLock is presented as a living product brief, not a long form or an unexplained matching score.

**Ways to start**
- A spoken idea: *"I want an organiser for this desk, with room for my accessories."*
- A photograph of the intended space, with explicit permission to use it for this project.
- An inspiration image or product link, with a note about what the customer likes and dislikes.
- A sketch or an existing product that needs a specific modification.

**The brief card**

| Field | Experience rule |
|---|---|
| Purpose | Describe the job the product should do in ordinary language. |
| Must-haves | Requirements that cannot change without explicit approval. |
| Preferences | Ranked wishes that may be traded off, with permission. |
| Dimensions and compatibility | Record units, source and confirmation status. |
| Budget | Separate target budget from an absolute spending limit. |
| Delivery | Location, desired date and whether timing is essential. |
| Unknowns | Questions still needing customer or seller evidence. |
| Acceptance checks | What the customer will inspect when the item arrives. |

**Clarification behaviour.** Ask the smallest useful next question and explain why it matters. If a
photograph suggests a dimension, label it unconfirmed and ask for a measurement. If two requirements
conflict, show the conflict before looking for offers. Allow "I do not know" and give a clear next
step instead of inventing a value.

**Changes and ownership.** A change creates a visible new brief version and shows which existing
offers may no longer apply. Seller suggestions remain proposals until accepted. The original
inspiration and previous approved versions remain reviewable.

**Customer-facing language.** Use "Must stay the same", "Flexible", "Needs checking" and "Changed
since your last approval". Avoid opaque percentages that imply certainty. If an overall suitability
label is shown, the requirement-by-requirement reasons stay accessible.

## 05 · Creative studio and product discovery

The studio encourages imagination while keeping the boundary between a concept and a real offer
unmistakable.

**Creative exploration.** A large central concept with a small set of meaningful variations. Customers
can mark an area and say "make this narrower", "keep this finish" or "remove this compartment".
Confirmed requirements stay visible beside the image. A visual edit does not silently alter the brief.

**Four visual states**
- **Concept visualisation** — illustrative only; availability and feasibility not confirmed.
- **Existing product** — a real listing, with source and checked details.
- **Supplier proposal** — a seller's offered design, with open questions and conditions.
- **Approved sample** — evidence of a specific sample accepted for a specific brief version.

**Discovery and inventory.** Results show item, seller, relevant specs, delivery eligibility, stock
status, total-cost completeness and when availability was checked. Distinguish available, low stock,
out of stock, made to order and unknown. A stock observation is not a reservation; if a seller offers
one, show its terms and expiry separately.

**Result organisation.** Group results as **Meets confirmed requirements**, **Possible with a change**
and **Not suitable**, explaining the decisive difference. A cheaper item that violates a must-have is
never promoted as the best option. Rejected options stay inspectable.

**Incomplete information.** Use "Delivery not yet confirmed" or "Material needs seller confirmation".
With no match: *"No suitable match found in the sources checked."* Offer to expand the search, change
a flexible preference, request sourcing or ask a maker. Never imply an invented visual is purchasable.

**Respectful inspiration.** Support original functional briefs and permitted reference use. Do not
promise exact copies of protected designs or remove branding to disguise imitation; redirect to an
original alternative with similar functional goals.

## 06 · Buy, source or make

Every route uses the same customer brief, so comparison stays about the outcome.

| Route | What the customer sees | Before commitment |
|---|---|---|
| Buy existing | Listing, fit explanation, stock observation and delivery terms. | Confirm the selected item and seller's final commercial terms. |
| Source elsewhere | Sourcing partner, origin, quoted costs, delivery assumptions and return limitations. | Resolve unknown charges and obtain a clear sourcing proposal. |
| Customise | Base product, permitted modifications and revised specifications. | Approve the modification scope and resulting acceptance checks. |
| Make new | Maker proposal, feasibility conditions, sample plan and minimum quantity. | Approve the exact specification, quote and production terms. |

**Offer comparison.** Compare must-have compliance, proposed changes, item cost, customisation,
shipping, disclosed fees, unresolved charges, estimated delivery, sample requirements and aftercare.
Distinguish seller estimates from commitments. Never sum incomparable figures into a misleading
"all-in" total while charges remain unknown.

**Inventory changes during consideration.** If an item sells out or a quote expires, mark the offer
unavailable for approval and keep it as history. Explain which alternatives remain valid. Never switch
sellers, products, quantities or prices without approval. Offer a restock alert or custom quote.

**Offer readiness.** Three states: **Ready to review**, **Needs clarification**, **Not suitable**. A
ready offer still needs approval. Unknown essential dimensions, or a delivery condition that conflicts
with a firm deadline, prevent an offer being presented as meeting the brief.

**The no-route outcome.** Show why fulfilment is blocked: missing information, unavailable stock, no
willing maker, budget conflict or unverified feasibility. Let the customer save, change a flexible
condition, seek another seller or close. An honest dead end beats a fabricated success.

## 07 · Seller workspace and quote collaboration

Turns a vague wish into a structured commercial conversation without forcing acceptance of impossible
requirements.

**Request inbox.** Each card shows category, brief summary, confirmed dimensions, delivery region,
budget visibility preference, quantity, essential constraints and open questions. Sellers can accept
for assessment, ask a question, decline with a reason or propose an alternative. Assessment is not
acceptance of an order.

**Brief review.** A clean visual plus a requirement checklist. Sellers mark each requirement
**achievable**, **needs clarification**, **proposed change** or **not achievable**, and can attach
material information, drawings or sample evidence. They are never asked to infer hidden dimensions
from decorative images.

**Quote contents**
- Exact brief version, quantity, material, finish, dimensions and included accessories.
- Item price, design or sample charges, shipping, disclosed fees and any unresolved charges.
- Lead-time estimate, delivery assumptions, quote validity and minimum quantity.
- Sample process, approval checkpoints, cancellation terms and defect/remediation terms.
- Every proposed deviation, with a reason and the customer decision required.

**Counteroffers.** Original requirement and proposed change side by side. A seller may offer a
narrower compartment, different material or later date, but the customer must see the impact.
Clarifications and quotes stay tied to their brief versions; if the brief changes, the seller
reconfirms affected terms.

**Seller fulfilment view.** After approval: accepted specification, milestones, sample decisions and
open issues. Sellers can report a delay, request a change or submit progress evidence, but cannot
overwrite the approved design. Changes affecting scope, price, appearance, compatibility or timing
trigger a customer decision request.

**Fair participation.** Never manufacture seller availability or imply an external seller agreed to
participate. In the demonstration, distinguish participating sellers from fictional sample sellers.
Never expose competing sellers' confidential quotes to one another.

## 08 · Approval, fulfilment and aftercare

The approved brief becomes the shared reference for delivery and resolution.

**Approval screen.** A focused review, not a celebratory shortcut: chosen design, confirmed
essentials, accepted compromises, total known price, unresolved charges, delivery terms, sample
requirement and seller terms. A clear final action such as **"Approve this specification and quote"**
that accurately states whether money is being committed.

**Approval safeguards**
- No purchase, deposit, sample charge or seller commitment without explicit authorisation.
- No approval of a stale quote or a brief version that has materially changed.
- No unresolved must-have presented as satisfied.
- Changes after approval require a new decision, not a hidden update.

**Fulfilment timeline.** Only relevant stages: order acknowledged, sample preparation, sample review,
production, ready to ship, shipped, delivered, acceptance review. Estimates marked as estimates. Show
the next milestone and who must act. A sample rejection returns to revision; it never auto-authorises
production.

**Delivery acceptance.** A checklist drawn from the approved brief: dimensions, finish, included parts,
visible condition and fit. Customers may upload evidence or request help. Delivery confirmation is not
acceptance of conformity; a photo does not prove every quality characteristic.

**Exceptions and resolution.** Preserve the approved spec, seller communications and customer
evidence. Offer the next steps the agreed terms allow: clarification, rework proposal, replacement,
cancellation or dispute review. Never promise automatic refunds, escrow or legal outcomes unless
actually offered.

**Close the loop.** On acceptance, save the final item record and care information. Allow reorder or
modification from the accepted spec, with fresh availability, pricing and seller confirmation. Ask for
feedback on requirement fulfilment, not just how attractive the concept image was.

## 09 · Optional shared demand

Shared demand is a route to feasibility, not a prerequisite for personal customisation.

**When to offer it.** Only when a minimum quantity or setup cost blocks an otherwise credible
proposal. Explain the individual custom option first if one exists. Opt-in, with clear visibility of
what is shared and whether payment or reservation is involved.

**Compatibility before popularity.** A batch needs compatible manufacturing essentials. Similar-looking
requests may have incompatible dimensions, materials or performance needs. Show common specifications
and allowed personal variations separately. Never merge customers into one design because images look
alike.

**Customer view**
- Shared base design and the customer's own permitted variation.
- Seller-confirmed minimum quantity and current eligible commitments.
- Price conditions, decision date and what happens if the threshold is missed.
- Clear labels: interest, non-binding reservation and paid commitment are different states.
- Withdrawal conditions and the effect of specification changes on the commitment.

**Batch launch gate.** Before a batch proceeds, reconfirm seller willingness, common spec, eligible
quantity, commercial terms and individual approvals. Reaching a threshold does not prove feasibility.
If terms change, customers can accept the new version or withdraw under disclosed terms.

**Privacy and fairness.** Aggregated progress only — never other participants' addresses, images or
requests. No fabricated demand, fake countdowns or implied guaranteed savings. Explain exclusions and
offer an individual route where available.

**Initial demonstration boundary.** Pooling is a secondary branch or future feature. The primary demo
succeeds for one customer and one maker. If pooling is shown, use clearly labelled fictional
participants with no implied purchase commitments.

## 10 · Cross-device and pluggable experience

One project travels with the customer; each surface contributes what it suits.

| Surface | Best product role | Experience boundary |
|---|---|---|
| Desktop or laptop | Full workspace, comparisons, seller quotes and approvals. | Keep dense details readable; preserve the customer's place. |
| Phone | Capture, voice, measurements, decisions and delivery checks. | One clear next action and compact summaries. |
| Tablet | Visual exploration, annotations and side-by-side review. | Touch support and an optional larger creative workspace. |
| TV | Shared inspiration and household comparison. | Hide private details; hand purchasing to a private device. |
| Smart glasses | User-started capture and short spoken clarification. | No continuous monitoring; approvals move to a suitable screen. |
| Watch | Status, reminders and handoff prompts. | Never compress specs or spending approval into a tiny alert. |
| Retail or maker site | Start from an item or request a modification in context. | Clearly show when the customer enters or leaves DemandFoundry. |

**Pluggable behaviour.** Standalone workspace, embedded retailer panel, maker "request customisation"
entry point or share-to-project action — all lead to the same brief and history. The customer always
knows which business is making an offer and who is responsible for fulfilment.

**Handoff design.** "Continue on my phone" / "Open this project on desktop" opens the exact pending
decision with a short recap. No duplicate orders or conflicting approvals. Concurrent edits surface a
"brief changed" notice and ask the approver to review the latest version.

**Capability honesty.** Explain device limits and hand off gracefully. Label simulated surfaces in a
prototype. Shared-screen mode hides address, payment and private seller discussions. Convenience never
weakens consent or purchasing controls.

## 11 · Visual identity and interaction design

A calm design atelier connected to a trustworthy commerce workspace — not a crowded marketplace or an
endless chat thread.

**Design direction.** Warm off-white backgrounds, deep ink typography, restrained **teal** accents and
generous spacing. Product imagery prominent, controls secondary. **Amber** for unresolved information,
**red** for blocked requirements — always with text and an icon. No decorative effects that obscure
dimensions, prices or status.

**Information hierarchy.** Every stage answers: *What am I creating? What is confirmed? What changed?
What do I do next?* Keep project name, brief version and current stage visible. Progressive disclosure
for evidence and seller terms, but never hide material costs or deviations.

**Signature desktop layout**
- Left: project stages, saved versions and activity.
- Centre: concept, selected product or supplier proposal, with contextual annotations.
- Right: intent card, confirmed requirements, unresolved questions and next decision.
- Bottom/contextual panel: evidence, comparison and conversation, without replacing the workspace.

**Signature interactions.** A **requirement pin** marks "keep this". A **change lens** highlights
differences between two versions. A **fulfilment lens** switches between buy, source and make while
keeping the same brief. A **decision card** explains a proposed compromise and its consequence before
accept/reject.

**Motion and feedback.** Subtle transitions connect a visual edit to a brief change. Honest progress
during search or seller review, and the user can leave the screen. Never simulate completed checks with
decorative progress bars. Confirmations state what was saved, sent or approved — not just "Success".

**Accessibility.** Readable type, keyboard navigation, descriptive labels, sufficient contrast,
captions/text alternatives for voice, reduced motion. Colour, speech or precise dragging is never the
only way to complete a task. A text-based brief and comparison view sits alongside images.

| Home / project hub | Start options, pending decisions, active projects. | Start or resume a project. |
| Capture | Input choices, privacy explanation, reference preview. | Use this inspiration. |
| Brief builder | Purpose, must-haves, preferences, limits, unknowns. | Confirm my brief. |
| Creative studio | Labelled concepts, variations, pinned requirements. | Explore fulfilment routes. |
| Route explorer | Buy, source, customise and make, with readiness. | Review an option. |
| Offer detail | Fit checklist, evidence, availability and terms. | Ask a question or shortlist. |
| Request review | What sellers receive, recipient choice, privacy. | Send approved request. |
| Quote inbox | New offers, pending questions, expired quotes. | Compare offers. |
| Comparison | Requirement differences, costs and delivery conditions. | Choose an offer to review. |
| Change review | Before/after, affected requirements, consequences. | Accept change or keep original. |
| Final approval | Exact version, price, terms and authorisation. | Approve the stated commitment. |
| Fulfilment | Milestones, evidence, exceptions and next action. | Review sample or update. |
| Delivery check | Acceptance checklist and issue evidence. | Accept or report an issue. |
| Seller workspace | Brief, feasibility response, quote and milestones. | Submit a structured response. |

**Shared controls.** Every project screen offers save, back, help and a visible current stage.
Risk-bearing actions use explicit labels. Leaving a screen never implies consent. The approved version
can be inspected without accidentally editing it.

**Empty and loading states.** An empty quote inbox explains no seller has responded yet and offers a
next step. A search in progress names the route being explored without inventing results. A failed
action preserves the draft and states whether anything was actually sent or committed.

| Core | Buy/source/make comparison | Choose a credible route against the same brief. |
| Core | Seller request and quote workspace | Receive structured proposals and clarification. |
| Core | Deviation review and approval | Approve a specific change, not a vague substitute. |
| Core | Project continuity | Resume the same decision on desktop and phone. |
| Core | Fulfilment and acceptance record | Track the agreed item through delivery and issues. |
| Expansion | Optional shared demand | Pool compatible requests when a batch is useful. |
| Expansion | Embedded retail and maker entry points | Start a project where inspiration is discovered. |

| Expansion | Household collaboration | Share inspiration without sharing spending authority. |

**Demonstration scope.** One non-electrical desk organiser; one customer; a small labelled catalog; one
maker workspace; a requirement conflict; one revised quote; desktop-to-phone continuity; a sandbox
approval; and a delivery-check preview. Other device surfaces may appear as clearly labelled
simulations.

**Do not add to the initial experience.** Universal category coverage, automatic purchases, guaranteed
instant manufacturing, autonomous refunds, hidden subscriptions, social feeds, speculative carbon
counters, unsupported quality scores, complex bidding games or unnecessary virtual worlds. A feature
count must not replace the core proof: **intent survives the journey to an actionable offer.**

## 14 · Demonstration storyboard

One coherent story with a visible conflict and a credible resolution. All values are fictional
demonstration inputs.

**Scenario.** Ashfaque wants a compact desk organiser inspired by a visual concept. Confirmed maximum
depth **18 cm**, **no drilling**, target budget **INR 3,000**. The customer confirms these values; they
are not inferred from a photograph.

| Beat | What the audience sees | What it proves |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Inspiration | Capture a desk image and describe the desired organiser. | A natural start, not a catalog search form. |
| 2. Intent | Confirm depth, mounting constraint and budget. | The product understands what cannot change. |
| 3. Concept | Refine a labelled visualisation. | Creativity remains separate from availability. |
| 4. Existing stock | A visually attractive item is rejected: it is 22 cm deep. | Similarity does not override the brief. |
| 5. Maker request | Send the customer-approved brief to a demo seller. | Unmet demand becomes actionable. |
| 6. Counteroffer | A maker proposes 20 cm depth; the system flags the conflict. | Supplier convenience does not rewrite intent. |
| 7. Resolution | The maker offers a revised compartment layout at 18 cm. | A visible compromise preserves the essential constraint. |
| 8. Device handoff | Continue the exact quote review on a phone surface. | Continuity is about the decision, not duplicated screens. |
| 9. Approval | Review the exact specification and create a sandbox order. | The customer controls the commercial commitment. |
| 10. Aftercare | Preview delivery checks against the approved brief. | The promise survives beyond checkout. |

**Presenter narrative.** *"Most shopping experiences help me buy what exists. DemandFoundry helps me
preserve what I actually need. When stock does not fit, it creates a clear brief for someone who can
make or source it. And when that offer changes, it asks me before my idea changes."*

**Demonstration honesty.** Label sample catalog entries, fictional sellers, staged responses, simulated
devices and sandbox orders. Never claim a real supplier response, live payment, actual stock
reservation or completed delivery unless it truly occurred. End with the approved outcome and the
remaining real-world conditions, not a fabricated success metric.

## 15 · Failure states, trust and customer control

| Situation | Required experience |
|---|---|
| Unclear measurement | Ask for confirmation; block fit claims until resolved. |
| Conflicting requirements | Explain the conflict and ask which flexible preference may change. |
| No suitable stock | State the checked-source limit and offer sourcing or making. |
| Stale availability | Recheck or mark unknown; do not imply a reservation. |
| Seller does not respond | Show the status honestly and offer another seller or pause. |
| Quote exceeds budget | Explain the cost drivers; do not raise the limit silently. |
| Unexpected charge | Expose it and require renewed review before commitment. |
| Material change after approval | Pause the affected commitment and request a new decision. |
| Device cannot complete a task | Provide a private, context-preserving handoff. |
| Duplicate action or conflicting edits | Show the latest state; prevent a second implied commitment. |
| Delivery mismatch | Open an evidence-led issue review against the approved version. |
| No feasible route | Explain why, preserve the project and allow closure without pressure. |

**Privacy and consent.** Ask permission for capture and seller sharing separately. Let customers review
or remove sensitive imagery before sending a brief. Clear project deletion and sharing controls. Explain
any retention needed for an active order or dispute; never promise deletion that cannot be honoured.

**Notifications.** Prioritise material events: a question needs answering, an offer changed, a sample
needs review, delivery is affected. Customer chooses channels and frequency. No repeated purchase
pushes; no private details broadcast to a shared TV or watch.

**Customer agency.** The customer can reject a change, choose another seller, pause or decide not to
buy. Explain consequences without false urgency. A saved concept, quote request or shared-demand
interest is never equated with a purchase.

## 16 · Success criteria and final product direction

Judge the product on preserved intent and credible fulfilment — not the number of generated images or
how clever the conversation seems.

**Experience acceptance checklist**
- [ ] A new customer can identify the next action without reading a long chat history.
- [ ] Every concept, listing, supplier proposal and sample has an unmistakable status.
- [ ] Every must-have has a visible state: confirmed, unmet or unresolved.
- [ ] A seller counteroffer cannot silently change an essential requirement.
- [ ] Every quote is linked to the exact brief version it addresses.
- [ ] Unknown charges, availability and delivery conditions remain visible.
- [ ] Cross-device continuation opens the same project and pending decision.
- [ ] No spending or production commitment occurs without the stated approval.
- [ ] A no-match outcome remains honest and useful.
- [ ] Delivery review uses the same acceptance criteria approved before ordering.

**Measures to validate.** Brief completion, unresolved requirements, request-to-quote conversion,
suitable-quote acceptance, requirement changes, fulfilment exceptions and delivered-item acceptance.
Commercially: completed-order contribution after service and dispute costs. Compare against an
ordinary search-and-enquiry journey with consented user testing. Never present targets as achieved.

**Questions before expanding.** Will customers pay for preserved intent and sourcing effort? Will
makers respond to the structured brief? Which category has clear enough acceptance checks? Can quoted
delivery and total costs be established reliably? Do customers understand concept vs offer? Does
device handoff remove effort rather than add spectacle?

**Final direction.** Protect the customer's imagination through the messy commercial middle — missing
stock, incomplete specs, seller compromises, quote changes and delivery uncertainty. Keep the interface
visually inspiring but the decision process calm, explicit and trustworthy.

> *DemandFoundry: turn what you imagine into a clear, verifiable brief, then find a credible way to
> buy, source or make it without losing what mattered to you.*
